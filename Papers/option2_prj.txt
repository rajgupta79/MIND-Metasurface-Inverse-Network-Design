Metamaterials exhibit optical properties not observed in traditional materials. Such behavior emerges from the
interaction of light with precisely engineered subwavelength features built from different constituent materials.
Recent research into the design and fabrication of metamaterial-based devices has established a foundation
for the next generation of functional materials. Of particular interest is the all-dielectric metasurface, a two-
dimensional metamaterial exploiting shape-dependent resonant features while avoiding losses through the use of
dielectric building blocks. However, even this simple metamaterial class has a nearly inﬁnite number of possible
conﬁgurations; researchers now require new methods to efﬁciently explore these types of design spaces. In this
work, we employ rigorous coupled wave analysis to calculate reﬂection and transmission spectra associated
with a class of open-cylinder all-dielectric metasurface. By altering the geometric parameters of open-cylinder
metasurfaces, we generate a sparse training data set and construct artiﬁcial neural networks capable of relating
metasurface geometries to reﬂection and transmission spectra. Here, we successfully demonstrate that pseudo
autodecoder neural networks can suggest device geometries based on a requested optical performance—inverting
the design process for this metasurface class. As an example, we query for and discover a particular open-cylinder
metasurface displaying a reﬂection band R ⩾99% centered at λ0 = 1550 nm that is much broader λ = 450 nm
than anything reported for optical metasurfaces. We then analyze the modal interplay in the open-cylinder
metasurface to better understand the underlying physics driving the broadband behavior. Ultimately, we conclude
that neural networks are ideally suited for generally approaching these types of complex inverse design problems.

I. INTRODUCTION
Metamaterials exhibit novel properties due to ﬁne-tuned
light-matter interactions with subwavelength scatterers. Even
though such devices were theorized long ago [1], only recently
have devices with feature sizes relevant to infrared and vis-
ible wavelengths become experimentally viable [2]. To date,
various bottom-up and top-down fabrication approaches have
driven the design and fabrication of these structures depend-
ing on the constituent materials in use and the geometrical
arrangements sought after [3–8]. Metamaterials demonstrate
potential as compact optical components in a variety of appli-
cations, including the design and manufacture of plasmonic
metasurfaces [9,10], solar sails [11], optical absorbers [12,13],
near-perfect reﬂectors [14–16], and beam collimators [17].
Simpler metamaterials have intriguing optical properties as
well. The two-dimensional metasurface, for example, is a
subclass composed of a single patterned layer and has been in-
vestigated for a variety of applications [18–20]. As a particular
example, the all-dielectric metasurface [21–25] can possess
desirable performance with regards to highly reﬂective low-
loss devices and can even be harnessed in tunable systems
[26,27].
Even a relatively simple metasurface has a near-inﬁnite
design space in terms of the possible ways to conﬁgure the
*matthew.mills.25@us.af.mil
constituent materials and surface geometry. This is further
complicated by the incoming radiation deﬁned by its ele-
vation and azimuthal angle of incidence, polarization, beam
shape, wavelength, and even nonlinear interactions [28]—
all of which affect the response of the metasurface device.
Even with the most modern high-performance computing re-
sources and optimized computational electromagnetic meth-
ods (CEMs), one simply cannot exhaustively search the mas-
sive parameter spaces in question. Because a comprehensive
“brute-force” search is untenable, any attempts at creating
a metamaterial with a targeted performance must leverage
an understanding of the underlying physics. For example,
Mie scattering concepts [15,29,30], Fano resonance behaviors
[6,24,26,31], and adjoint methods [32] have been used to
design particular subsets of device geometries. However, there
exists no prescription for inverting Maxwell’s equations; thus,
one cannot generally design an arbitrary metamaterial starting
with targeted optical constraints. In a few limited cases, tradi-
tional optimization routines are capable of designing meta-
surfaces with desired optical properties [33–36]; however,
each new targeted property and/or device geometry must be
handled individually, ultimately limiting the overall efﬁciency
of the discovery process. As an example, genetic algorithms
can be implemented to ﬁne-tune design parameters but tend
to struggle in higher-dimensional searches, often getting stuck
in the local minima of the large parameter space. As a result,
efﬁciently approaching this inverse design problem continues
to be a subject of considerable interest [37].

Machine learning (ML) methods have recently risen in
prominence to more judiciously handle the metasurface in-
verse design problem [7,38–43]. Artiﬁcial neural networks
(ANNs) [44] are particularly well suited for this task because
they are function approximators: a neural network learns a
multifunctional relationship⃗y = f (⃗x) when trained on input
data⃗x and output data⃗y. If an inverse relationship between the
input and output data exists, the neural network may instead
be trained to learn the inverse relationship⃗x = f −1( f (⃗x)) =
g(⃗y) [45]. In fact, recent work combining CEMs and ML
has now provided a pathway for exploring these kinds of
higher-dimensional inverse problems [7,38,39,46].
In this work, we consider a system of nanoscale Si open
cylinders [47] on a SiO2 substrate. This geometry is a gener-
alized version of a recently investigated all-dielectric solid-
cylinder metasurface with an experimentally demonstrated
broadband near-perfect reﬂection in the short-wave infrared
regime (R > 98% across a λ = 200 nm wide bandwidth
covering λ0 = 1550 nm) [16]. With this previous work serving
as a precedent, this paper seeks to demonstrate the power
of ANNs for inverse metamaterial design by querying for an
all-dielectric open-cylinder metasurface that out performs the
previously considered solid-cylinder metasurface in terms of
both broadband reﬂection and peak reﬂectivity. We calculate
the reﬂection and transmission spectra of open-cylinder meta-
surfaces randomly sampled from a four-dimensional (4D)
input parameter space using rigorous coupled-wave analysis
(RCWA). The output of these simulations serves as training
data sets for ANNs built to suggest an inverse design solution.
The most predictive success is observed with an ANN trained
in a pseudo autodecoder (PAD) neural network architecture
[48]. We then utilize the PAD-trained neural network to
uncover a highly reﬂective, broadband open-cylinder meta-
surface centered around the telecommunications wavelength
λ0 = 1550 nm exhibiting R ⩾99% over twice the bandwidth
λ = 450 nm previously reported. Following the work of
Ref. [22], we then analyze the modes arising within the open-
cylinder metasurface to better understand the origin of the
highly reﬂective broadband behavior. We conclude that al-
though this method could be used, it becomes untenable with
increasing metasurface complexity. Thus, the ML protocols
deﬁned in this work provide a more robust and general method
in designing metasurfaces, avoiding the need to derive ad hoc
solutions for individual geometries. The PAD ANN approach
accelerates the overall design process and enables the rapid
discovery of tailored functional materials.
II. METASURFACE ANNs: TRAINING AND
ARCHITECTURE

array of Si open cylinders on a SiO2 substrate surrounded
by air, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The computational electro-
magnetic method of choice is RCWA since it is well suited
for simulating such a periodic device [22]. RCWA efﬁciently
models the Floquet boundary conditions in the x and y
directions while avoiding any approximations to Maxwell’s
equations. This allows for computationally inexpensive ex-
traction of metasurface modes and reﬂection and transmission
coefﬁcients. We use the open source S4 [49] RCWA package
to simulate our metasurfaces coupled with the SIGNAC data

We construct and train the ANNs with our curated data
on a single Nvidia RTX-2080Ti GPU using the KERAS [52]
library with the TENSORFLOW [53] back end. We reserve 10%
of the data for performance evaluation after training, reserving
an additional 10% for validation during training, using a batch
size of 1024. The ANN is trained using the KERAS imple-

values for the ADAM hyperparameters (learning_rate = 0.001,
beta_1 = 0.9, beta_2 = 0.999, amsgrad = False) and with
the mean square error loss function. The ANNs train for up to
2000 generations with early stopping criteria loss < 10−9.
All ANNs utilized in this work use dense layers with the
rectiﬁed linear unit (ReLU) activation function, except for
the use of the sigmoid activation function in the ﬁnal layer,
limiting the output to the range [0, 1].

There are two essential advantages of training an SPN. The
ﬁrst advantage is with sufﬁcient training data, the SPN begins
to coarsely mimic the computational outputs of RCWA for
the range of open cylinders considered in Table I. This is
true mimicry in the sense that the SPN does not really learn
RCWA but instead is able to draw overarching mathematical
patterns which correlate the input parameter values with the
output reﬂection and transmission spectra. This important
distinction makes the spectral outputs of the SPN better suited
for solving the inverse design problem. The second advantage
is an adequately trained SPN is able to compute additional
output spectra many times faster than the original CEM (in
our case, the SPN is about 1 million times faster than RCWA).
As long as the training data are sufﬁcient, these two features
are ultimately what allow ANNs to converge for much larger
parameter spaces.

trends connecting the input parameter space to the output

from another perspective, consider the ﬁner spectral details
observed in the RCWA runs [e.g., Fig. 2(b)] which often have
oscillations around an average value. If the DPN is trained on
many sets of such data, it will try to account for these features,
ultimately overcorrecting the general trends in an attempt to
capture these ﬂuctuations.
The PAD not only delivers a SPN and PDPN combination
with incredible predictive capability but also provides a way
to interpret the predictions of an ANN via the SPN portion
of the network like the pseudo autoencoder considered by Liu
et al. [38]. By inverting and stitching together the now trained
PDPN and SPN networks, we can create an inverse design
pipeline: a user-deﬁned spectrum [Fig. 4(a)] is ﬁrst supplied to
the PDPN [Fig. 4(b)], designing a device with reﬂection and
transmission spectra that best meet the target criterion. The
PDPN-predicted design is then fed into the SPN, delivering a
near-instantaneous prediction of the device’s performance, as
shown in Fig. 4(c). It is important to realize that the PDPN
will not necessarily prescribe a design that exactly ﬁts the
user-deﬁned spectrum. Instead, it will deliver a design that
both exists within the parameter space on which it was trained
and best ﬁts the target features: one is guaranteed to obtain
an open-cylinder design within the range of Table I whose
reﬂection and transmission features are closest to targeted
performance. This guarantee proves to be extremely useful
when the training data ranges are preconstrained by fabrica-
tion tolerances.
The utility of this inverted PAD network is demonstrated
by the choice of target spectra shown in Fig. 4. By querying
for an open-cylinder square lattice device with broadband
high reﬂectivity centered around λ0 = 1550 nm, a candidate is
found with device parameters ro = 279 nm, ri = 77 nm, h =
593 nm, |⃗L| = 758 nm. When studying this predicted device
with a high-resolution RCWA simulation, we ﬁnd that the
predicted performance meets and even exceeds the target cri-
teria: λ ⩾450 nm, λ0 = 1550 nm, λ/λ0 > ±12.5% with
individual reﬂection peaks with T ⩽10−4 [Fig. 4(d)]. This
same procedure is repeated with the hexagonal lattice train-
ing data. A similar broadband device is uncovered, achiev-
ing T (1.25 μm ⩽λ ⩽1.75 μm) < 10−1 with geometry ro =
259 nm, ri = 72 nm, h = 571 nm, |⃗L| = 743 nm.

over the region of broadband reﬂection. Moreover, we ﬁnd
the individual modal proﬁles are wavelength dependent [see
Figs. 5(e) and 5(f)]. The total number of modes, the shape of
the modes, and which modes are active at any given λ are thus
a function of λ, making the task of orchestrating these modes
to create the reﬂection spectra observed in Figs. 5(c) and 5(d)
extremely onerous for all but the simplest geometries.
Previous research suggested it may be possible to orches-
trate modal contributions to interfere with the incoming wave,
resulting in nearly 100% reﬂection [56]. However, this was
considered in a two-mode regime geometry, whereas the two
systems we are considering convey the interplay of ﬁve or
more at any given wavelength. Careful management of the
interference of each mode within the metasurface to design
a geometry with target performance is infeasible. Indeed,
attempting such a solution as the design space increases
to include not only the open-cylinder geometry but more
complicated Bravais lattices, unit cells containing multiple
meta-atoms, and different constituent materials is simply im-
possible. We conclude that the ANN-based solution to the
inverse design of highly reﬂecting metasurface devices is both
more achievable and more appropriate than an approach that
requires deliberate handling of the active eigenmodes.
V. CONCLUSION
Metamaterials are a strong candidate for the design and
fabrication of the next generation of multifunctional materials.
Even with this limited simple design, the near-inﬁnite number
of available conﬁgurations makes exploring the parameter
space a daunting task. However, by judiciously building and
training ANNs, we showed a general pathway to solving
the inverse design problem for metasurfaces. A PAD-trained
PDPN was capable of advising open-cylinder metasurface de-
signs by prescription. This network was then used to uncover
an open-cylinder metasurface demonstrating high reﬂectivity
R ⩾99% with λ = 450 nm of bandwidth, centered around a
target wavelength λ0 = 1550 nm. This performance exceeded
the reﬂection bandwidths of comparable devices reported in
previous studies. Modal analysis of this broadband design
conveyed the complicated interaction of two to ten signiﬁcant
modes over the range of broadband reﬂection. From this,
we claimed that ANNs provide a more efﬁcient and general
way for designing metasurfaces. Additionally, attempting to
negotiate reﬂection properties by tracking modal interplay be-
comes infeasible for anything but the simplest cases. Consid-
ering other metasurfaces patterns is the immediate extension
of this research, as well as considering different materials
(other dielectrics, polymers), Bravais lattices, and complex
unit cells.
While we saw sufﬁcient predictive performance, the gen-
eral pipeline for generating training data, the choice of neu-
ral network architecture, and the training parameters of the
neural networks can all be subject to further examination. For
example, different architectures could decrease the amount of
training data necessary for convergence, or transfer learning
methods could be implemented to encompass multiple types
of metasurfaces into a singular ANN. All ANN-predicted
designs can be further optimized by hybridizing existing
optimization routines into the protocol or by directly factoring
in manufacturing-related constraints. Finally, initial attempts
utilizing modern electron-beam lithography and reactive ion
etching techniques have shown the proposed open-cylinder
geometries are realizable. Future work will involve scaling the
manufacture process and assessing the optical performance of
the fabricated metasurfaces.
